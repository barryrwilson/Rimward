## What this set teaches

- **The nuclear OTV front face is a tank cluster.** N-105 shows seven equal spherical tanks in hexagonal close-pack, surrounded by a wide flat annular radiation shield. This is not one big tank — it is a family of equal-diameter pressure vessels. Use exactly this packing geometry for any freighter propulsion section. The number of tanks and the shield disk diameter together communicate vehicle scale instantly.

- **Separate hab, tank, and propulsion into three visible zones.** Both the 1960 Mars ship (N-076) and the nuclear shuttle diagram (N-116) place the crew or payload at the bow, propellant tanks mid-ship, and the engine at the stern. Do not blend these zones. Make each one readable as a distinct silhouette segment. The distance between segments is what makes a vehicle feel large.

- **The delta-wing cutter form is fully documented.** N-108 and N-112 give the same vehicle from two angles. The key proportions are: ~5:1 body-length to wing-span, a very narrow pointed nose, a swept leading edge that begins at the nose and ends at mid-body, and a single tall fin. The mid-body cargo truss is open lattice, not a fairing. The engine bells are at the very tail. Reproduce these specific ratios, not a generic wing shape.

- **A radiation shield disk reads immediately as "nuclear."** Any large flat annular disk placed between the engine and the rest of the vehicle signals that something dangerous is being managed. No labels are needed. Use this in any faction where nuclear propulsion should be visible but not explained.

- **Radiator panels are the heat signature of a long mission.** N-077 and N-106 both show deployable flat radiator panels projecting at angles from the mid-ship spine. Their presence says "this vehicle runs hot for a long time" — it communicates deep-space or high-power operation without a label. Deploy them on any freighter or heavy with a nuclear or ion drive.

- **The space station truss grammar scales to a freighter spine.** ISS concepts (N-089 through N-094) show a long open truss backbone with modules at one end and power at the other. Translate this directly: a freighter's structural spine is long and readable on its own, with the cargo mass clustered at one end and the engine mass at the other. The truss itself is not filled in.

- **The 1975 station cutaway (N-098) shows internal density without external complexity.** The exterior cylinder is plain. The interior has seven decks. Apply this principle to any faction that hides internal richness: the surface language is faction-specific, but the suggestion of dense interior comes from window placement and vent or hatch density, not from surface greeble.

- **Modular two-cylinder tug architecture is a buildable grammar.** N-114 and N-115 both show a crew module cylinder joined end-to-end to a propulsion module cylinder, with a nose cone added for flight. The seam between the two cylinders is visible. This explicit joinline is not a flaw — it communicates replaceability and mission reconfiguration. Use it on Lamplighter cutters and heavies where swap-out components should read as designed, not broken.

- **Von Braun's wheel (N-097) is the one form that immediately reads as "artificial gravity."** If any in-game station or freighter habitation section uses a rotating ring, it will be immediately understood by players without explanation. The spoke-and-hub detail distinguishes it from a simple ring.

- **The octagonal multi-bell engine pod (N-106) is a direct heavy-class propulsion block.** Each octagonal housing holds five or six nozzle bells in a ring pattern. Two of these mounted symmetrically on a central body is all you need to communicate "heavy-duty thrusting machine." Use as the stern block for Lamplighter gate tug or Ferrous Hegemony bastion gunship.

- **The tug missions board (N-113) proves that one cutter-class design must work in four visual contexts.** The same delta-wing tug reads as an upper stage, a ferry, a payload launcher, and a station tender. Design cutters so the functional attachments (docking collar, payload cradle, service arm) are visible but do not change the silhouette beyond recognition. The vehicle's identity comes from the body form, not the mission hardware.

- **Mission architecture boards (N-107, N-116) confirm vehicle hierarchy by drawing all classes to the same schematic scale.** The tug is always smaller than the nuclear shuttle. The nuclear shuttle is always smaller than the station. Use these size ratios to validate the in-game class hierarchy against the real design vocabulary.
